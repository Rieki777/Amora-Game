# Legal Structures for Regenerative Land Projects

> How villages hold land, money, and liability: LLCs, cooperatives, community land trusts, 501(c)(3)/(c)(7) nonprofits, faith-based 508(c)(1)(A) organizations, private membership associations, and stewardship trusts. A founder reaches for this when the project moves from "friends on land" to real money, real deeds, and real disputes. **This is orientation, not legal advice. Rules vary by state and country, and the details (tax, securities, zoning, employment) are jurisdiction-specific. Always engage a lawyer licensed where the land sits before signing or filing anything.**

## Core patterns

**1. Match the entity to the function: most real communities run a stack, not one entity.**
No single structure does everything. The common working pattern: an LLC or co-op owns the residential land and handles member economics; a separate 501(c)(3) runs education, conservation, or charity; sometimes a CLT or trust holds the land underneath. Combining entities keeps charitable money and member benefit legally separated. Mixing them is the classic way to lose tax exemption. (Community Finders "7 Legal Structures"; FIC *Legal Structures for ICs*.)

**2. LLC: the default starting vehicle.**
Simple to form, cheap to maintain, pass-through taxation, and it shields members from personal liability on the land debt. The operating agreement is the real constitution: it can encode consent-based governance, capital accounts, buy-in/buy-out formulas, and what happens when someone leaves. Weaknesses: no tax-deductible donations, no charitable grants, and interests sold to passive investors can trigger securities law. Most forming communities start here and layer other entities later. (Community Finders; icmatch.org.)

**3. Housing cooperative: democratic ownership of the whole.**
The co-op corporation owns the property; members own shares plus an occupancy right. One member, one vote. **Limited-equity co-ops (LECs)** cap share resale price by formula (flat 1-3%, CPI, or area-median-income indexed) to keep housing permanently affordable. This is the dominant model in values-driven communities. **Group-equity co-ops** (the NASCO model) hold all equity collectively; members build none, which maximizes permanence and affordability. Main friction: conventional lenders rarely finance co-op shares ("share loans" are scarce), so co-ops lean on CDFIs, seller financing, and member loans. (Local Housing Solutions; NASCO equity handbook.)

**4. Community land trust (CLT): take the land off the market permanently.**
A nonprofit (usually 501(c)(3)) owns the land forever; residents own their homes/improvements and lease the ground on a ~99-year renewable lease. The ground lease sets a resale formula so homes stay affordable in perpetuity; classic CLT governance is a tripartite board (residents / surrounding community / public interest). Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac accept CLT ground leases via a "CLT Rider," so residents can still get mortgages. Trade-off: residents build limited equity, and running a CLT is a real organization with staff and compliance, not a paperwork trick. (Grounded Solutions Network; International Center for CLTs; farmlandaccess.org.)

**5. 501(c)(3): public benefit, with a hard catch for housing.**
Tax-exempt, donation-deductible, grant-eligible, but only for genuinely public purposes (education, conservation, relieving poverty). **Housing your own members is generally NOT charitable**: the IRS treats member housing as private benefit. The main safe harbor is low-income housing under Rev. Proc. 96-32 (roughly 75% of units to low-income occupants). So a village's 501(c)(3) typically runs the farm-education program or holds conservation land, while a separate member entity holds the homes. Assets are permanently dedicated to charity: if the org dissolves, members cannot take the land back. (IRS; Community Finders; Donorbox c3-vs-c7 guide.)

**6. 501(c)(7): the social-club option, honestly limited.**
For member-serving social/recreational organizations. Real constraints: exempt function is member-facing only, donations are NOT deductible, and per the IRS guideline no more than ~35% of gross receipts may come from outside the membership (within that, ~15% from nonmember use of facilities). Useful for a common-house/amenities association; useless for fundraising a land purchase. Practitioners disagree on whether (c)(7) is worth filing for at all for small communities. An unincorporated association or LLC often does the same job with less compliance. (Donorbox; LegalClarity PMA analysis.)

**7. 508(c)(1)(A). What it actually is: a filing exception, not a super-status.**
IRC §508(c)(1)(A) says churches, their integrated auxiliaries, and conventions/associations of churches are *excused from applying* (Form 1023) for 501(c)(3) recognition; they are automatically treated as 501(c)(3) if they actually qualify. That is the entire provision. It confers no tax advantage, protection, or "status" that ordinary 501(c)(3) recognition doesn't; anyone claiming otherwise is selling something (see #8). A "508 church" must still satisfy every substantive 501(c)(3) rule: organized and operated exclusively for religious purposes, no private inurement, no campaign intervention, insubstantial lobbying. It must also actually function as a church under the IRS's 14-factor facts-and-circumstances test (regular congregation, established worship, distinct religious history, ordained ministry, etc.; no single factor controls, but "we call our farm a ministry" fails). Churches do get real procedural protections (no Form 990; IRC §7611 audit procedures), and many legitimate churches still file Form 1023 voluntarily to reassure donors and access grants/discounts. (Simms Showers Law; Church Law Center; StartCHURCH; IRS "Churches Defined".)

**8. 508(c)(1)(A) promoter schemes: the biggest scam zone in this domain. Warn founders explicitly.**
A cottage industry sells "508 trusts," "free churches," and corporation-sole kits claiming: immunity from taxation and audits, exemption from employment/zoning/reporting law, automatic donor deductions, "no government jurisdiction." **All of these claims are false.** Courts have called the immunity argument frivolous (*Steeves v. IRS*, 2020); the IRS flagged corporation-sole abuse in Rev. Rul. 2004-27 and its "Dirty Dozen" list; DOJ has won injunctions and criminal convictions against promoters. Red flags: fees for a "package" promising you'll never file or owe taxes; advice to route personal expenses through the "church"; claims that labeling something religious exempts it from laws of general application. Downside for a real community: sham-church structuring risks back taxes, penalties, loss of donor deductibility, and poisons relations with regulators and neighbors. (LegalClarity "508 Trust Scam"; IRS IR-2004-42; DOJ press releases; Simms Showers.)

**9. Private membership associations (PMAs): narrow real protections, heavily oversold.**
Freedom of association is real (*Roberts v. U.S. Jaycees*, 1984) and a *genuinely* selective private club can be outside some public-accommodation rules. But courts look at substance (real screening, member governance, no effective public access), not the label on the paperwork. A PMA does **not**: exempt anyone from professional licensing, food-safety or FDA rules, employment/payroll law, or taxes (no automatic exemption; income is taxable unless the IRS grants a determination). The IRS has repeatedly warned about layering a PMA over an ordinary business to claim exemption. Legitimate use in a village: a members-only agreement for internal programs (e.g., a herd-share or wellness circle) as a *privacy and expectations* tool, never as a tax or licensing shield. (LegalClarity "Private Member Association"; Claimyr IRS analysis.)

**10. Trusts and easements for permanent land stewardship.**
Three practitioner-tested tools: **(a) Conservation easement**: landowner keeps title but permanently deeds away development rights to a conservation land trust; possible tax deduction; complex and irreversible; mismatched trusts (wildlife-focused vs. working farm) are a known problem. **(b) Agrarian Commons (Agrarian Trust)**: community-governed land-holding entities that buy farmland out of the market and issue long-term equitable leases for regenerative production; emerging model, ~14 commons formed; the national trust retains easements/repurchase options as mission insurance. **(c) Perpetual purpose trust / Oregon "stewardship trust"**: a non-charitable trust that owns land or a farm business *for a stated purpose*, with no beneficiaries (the Patagonia structure); fully supported in only a handful of states (DE, NH, WY, OR, ME, SD among them; Oregon's ORS 130.193 uses a stewardship committee); needs an enforcer, and a land conservancy is a natural pick. Purpose trusts remove the exit-sale temptation entirely, but the community gives up asset value forever. (farmlandaccess.org; Agrarian Trust FAQ; ABA "Patagonia, Purpose Trusts, and Stewardship Trusts".)

**11. International notes (orientation only; every country differs).**
UK: CLT movement has its own enabling statute and a national CLT Network; Community Interest Companies (CICs) and community benefit societies are common vehicles. Germany: the **Mietshäuser Syndikat** (~190 house projects) uses a dual-GmbH structure where the network holds a golden share blocking any resale: permanent decommodification without a trust; cloned in Austria (habiTAT), France, Netherlands. The transferable idea: a second entity holding a veto over sale is a cheap, durable anti-speculation lock. (syndikat.org; Wikipedia/MHS; Stadtbodenstiftung Berlin.)

## In practice

- **Phase it.** Year 0-1: unincorporated association or simple LLC with a strong operating agreement; get the deal done. Year 1-3: add the nonprofit for programs (use a fiscal sponsor for deductible donations while the 1023 is pending, typically 3-6 months). Year 3+: consider moving land into a CLT, co-op, or purpose trust once the community has proven stable.
- **Write the exit before the entrance.** Buy-in amount, buy-out formula, vesting, and dispute process go in the operating agreement or bylaws on day one. Most community legal blowups are member departures without a pre-agreed formula.
- **Keep two ledgers, two boards.** If you run a c3 next to a member LLC: separate bank accounts, separate minutes, arm's-length written agreements for anything shared (land lease, equipment). The c3 must never subsidize member living costs.
- **For faith-rooted communities:** if the spiritual life is real (regular worship, congregation, doctrine, ministry), 508(c)(1)(A) auto-exemption is legitimate, but document the church characteristics as if you'll be audited, comply with all 501(c)(3) rules, and consider voluntary Form 1023 for donor confidence. If the religion is a wrapper for tax goals, stop: that's the scam pattern in #8.
- **Financing reality check before choosing:** banks understand TICs, HOAs, and CLT-riders; they mostly don't understand co-op shares or exotic trusts. If members need mortgages, structure around what lenders in your state will actually underwrite (credit unions and CDFIs are the usual allies).
- **Zoning first.** Entity choice never overrides land use law. Check dwelling-count limits, ag-zoning restrictions, and septic/occupancy rules before closing. This kills more village plans than tax law does.

## Failure modes

- **The promoter package.** A founder pays $2-15k for a "508 church kit," "PMA bundle," or "sovereign trust" and believes the community is now tax- and regulation-immune. Early signs: talk of "private jurisdiction," refusal to get local counsel, routing personal expenses through the entity. Remedy: unwind it with a real attorney before the IRS or a state AG does it for you; the DOJ actively prosecutes promoters and the IRS pursues participants for back taxes and penalties.
- **Charitable trap.** Community puts the homes into its 501(c)(3) for grant access, then discovers members can't build equity and the assets can never come back out. Practitioners' fix: land in the nonprofit/CLT, *improvements* in member ownership via ground lease.
- **Auto-revocation.** Non-church exempt orgs that skip Form 990 filings three years running lose exemption automatically, no warning. Calendar it.
- **c7/PMA drift into public commerce.** The "members-only" farm store selling to everyone who signs a clipboard. Courts and the IRS look at substance; nonmember revenue past the ~35% line or zero real screening collapses the private status.
- **Single-signature land.** Title held by one founder "for now," then death, divorce, or a falling-out. Get title into the entity at closing, even an imperfect one.
- **Consensus governance welded into the deed.** Encoding full-consensus for every decision into bylaws or a ground lease makes routine action (refinance, roof repair) hostage to one holdout. Keep governance detail in amendable policy layers, not in recorded instruments.

## When NOT to use this

- **508(c)(1)(A):** if the community is not genuinely and primarily religious by the IRS's own criteria. Also skip if you want grant funding and institutional donors; they will demand a determination letter anyway.
- **PMA:** never as a tax, licensing, or food-safety strategy; only as a membership-contract layer inside an otherwise compliant structure.
- **501(c)(3):** if the main activity is housing the members, you'll fail the private-benefit test or trap the assets.
- **CLT / purpose trust:** if members expect to build meaningful home equity or the group may want to sell and relocate; these tools are one-way doors by design.
- **Co-op:** if members need conventional mortgages in a state with no share-loan lenders and no CDFI coverage.
- **Bare LLC forever:** once you take public donations, run educational programs, or hold conservation values, a for-profit shell invites tax on gifts and blocks grants.
