# Nonviolent Communication & Community Conflict Transformation

> Practitioners consistently rank conflict (not money, not zoning) as the leading killer of intentional communities. Diana Leafe Christian's research (interviews with dozens of successful and failed communities for *Creating a Life Together*) estimates roughly 90% of forming communities fail, "often in conflict and heartbreak," and the successful 10% all built communication skills, conflict agreements, and process structure *before* they needed them. A founder reaches for this domain when tension is surfacing (or being avoided), when designing membership and conflict agreements, or when one unresolved dispute is starting to consume the whole group.

## Core patterns

**1. Communities die of unresolved conflict, and the fix is structural, not heroic.**
Christian's core finding: the failed 90% made the same handful of mistakes (no shared written vision, no conflict agreements, no membership screening, no graduated consequences), while the successful 10% did the same five or six things right. Her line: "Forming a community is deeply psychological"; hidden expectations and emotional wounds ricochet through visioning sessions unless process structure contains them. (Diana Leafe Christian, *Creating a Life Together*, New Society Publishers / FIC.)

**2. OFNR: the four moves of Nonviolent Communication.**
Marshall Rosenberg's NVC (Center for Nonviolent Communication, cnvc.org) trains four components: **Observation** (what a video camera would record: "you arrived 20 minutes after the agreed time," not "you're disrespectful"), **Feeling** (inner emotional experience: "I feel frustrated," not the faux-feeling "I feel ignored," which is an interpretation of the other's behavior), **Need** (the universal value underneath: reliability, rest, belonging; never mentioning a specific person or action), **Request** (specific, doable, present-tense, with genuine openness to "no"). The premise: every action is an attempt to meet a human need; naming needs moves talk from blame to problem-solving. (CNVC; BayNVC "Basics of Nonviolent Communication.")

**3. The four distinctions carry the whole method.**
Observation vs. evaluation; feeling vs. thought/faux-feeling; need vs. strategy ("I want you at the work party" is a strategy; the need might be support or fairness; separating them opens up alternative strategies); request vs. demand (per BayNVC: a refused request leads to dialogue, a refused demand leads to punishment). Most NVC "failures" are one of these four collapsing.

**4. Empathy before problem-solving.**
NVC practice is symmetric: empathic guessing of the other's feelings/needs ("are you feeling X because you need Y?", with the other person always the final authority), and self-empathy before hard conversations. Practitioner consensus: solutions proposed before both parties feel heard get relitigated. (BayNVC; CNVC.)

**5. Diagnose which of three conflict types you have.**
Christian distinguishes (a) ordinary interpersonal conflict, (b) **structural conflict**, friction generated by missing systems, not bad people: minutes not posted (information asymmetry = informal power), no labor-tracking (burnout + resentment), vague mission statements (every proposal fight is secretly a vision fight), unequal land ownership ("feudal" landlord-tenant dynamics), and (c) **especially challenging behaviors** needing a consequences track, not more empathy. Treating structural conflict as interpersonal is a signature failure: you mediate two people while the system keeps manufacturing the fight. (Earthaven Ecovillage interview with Christian, "On Structural Conflict.")

**6. Match intervention to escalation stage (Glasl's ladder).**
Friedrich Glasl's nine-stage conflict escalation model, the standard escalation ladder in mediation literature, groups stages into three bands: **1-3 win-win** (hardening, debate, actions-not-words: self-help and facilitated dialogue still work), **4-6 win-lose** (coalitions, face-attacks, threats: needs outside mediation; the group starts recruiting sides), **7-9 lose-lose** (destruction becomes the goal: needs powerful third-party intervention or separation). Glasl frames escalation as a *descent* into more primitive forms of dispute. Practical rule for villages: interpersonal tools (NVC, mediation) reliably work only in stages 1-3; if members are recruiting allies, escalate the *response*: bring in an outside facilitator. (Glasl, *Konfliktmanagement*; summary by Thomas Jordan, "Glasl's Nine-Stage Model of Conflict Escalation.")

**7. Restorative Circles: justice as a system built before conflict.**
Dominic Barter's Restorative Circles (developed in Rio de Janeiro favelas, restorativecircles.org; taught via CNVC) have three phases: **pre-circle** (facilitator meets each participant separately, hears them, invites them), **circle** (three stages: mutual understanding, each person heard until they confirm they were understood; self-responsibility, owning one's part; agreed action, a concrete plan), **post-circle** (a scheduled follow-up after the action plan's last item, to review and celebrate or revise). Barter's key insight for communities: co-create the *restorative system* (who convenes, how anyone can call a circle, where it happens) in peacetime, so calling a circle is a normal community act, not an accusation. Dancing Rabbit Ecovillage uses Restorative Circles as its formal tier.

**8. Graduated consequences protect the community from its own niceness.**
Christian: design "a graduated series of fair, compassionate consequences, from mild to increasingly serious." Model sequence (from Community Alternatives Society, cited in *Creating a Life Together*): (1) one-on-one conversation requesting change → (2) four-person meeting, two from each "side" → (3) accountability committee / mediation → (4) written agreement with monthly check-ins → (5) community decision on membership status. Without this ladder, communities oscillate between tolerating harm for years and sudden explosive expulsion.

**9. Screening is conflict prevention.**
Christian: "If your community front door is difficult to enter, healthy people will strive to get in." Multi-stage membership (6-12+ months, provisional periods, community vetting at each stage) filters for the emotional maturity that every other tool in this file presumes. Saying "no" early is kinder than asking someone to leave later.

**10. Know NVC's limits: the in-tradition critique.**
Senior NVC trainer Miki Kashtan and others note: OFNR framing can locate problems in individual communication while obscuring power differences and structural causes; and "NVC-speak" can become jargon-policing ("that's an evaluation, not an observation"), reproducing the domination it aims to dissolve. NVC works best between rough equals; pair it with structural fixes (pattern 5) and governance (sociocracy, clear agreements), and treat it as one part of the toolkit. (Kashtan, "Transforming Power Relations," nonviolentcommunication.com.)

## In practice

**First 6 months of a forming community:**
- Write and adopt a **conflict agreement** before there is a conflict: a named escalation path, e.g. (1) direct 1:1 within two weeks of the incident → (2) supported conversation with a neutral third member → (3) mediation or the conflict team → (4) restorative circle → (5) community/consequences process. Members commit to it as a condition of joining.
- Adopt **communication agreements** for meetings (no interrupting, speak for yourself, how disagreement is voiced). Christian lists these among the highest-impact structures.
- Do a shared **NVC foundations training** (a weekend with a CNVC-certified trainer, or FIC's courses such as Laird Schaub's "Working with Conflict in Community" at ic.org) so the group has common vocabulary before the first real fight.

**Standing structures (steady state):**
- A 2-4 person **conflict resolution team** (Dancing Rabbit's model): keeps skills current, offers informal and formal mediation, convenes circles; Schaub similarly recommends a designated "care team."
- **Restorative system agreed in peacetime**: anyone can call a circle; the process is written down; facilitation rotates.
- Kill structural-conflict fuel on a schedule: minutes posted within a week, labor visible/tracked, mission statement concrete enough to settle proposal fights (Christian's Earthaven interview; she notes communities with labor requirements and tracking "tend to have higher morale and lower burn-out").
- Regular relational maintenance: sharing/check-in circles at a set cadence (weekly or biweekly), separate from business meetings. This keeps stage-1 friction from compounding.
- Feelings/needs lists (CNVC or Sociocracy For All versions) printed and physically present in meeting spaces; empathy-buddy pairs for practice.

**In the moment:** when conflict surfaces in a business meeting, name it, park the agenda item, and route it to the conflict path. Don't litigate relationships in a decision meeting (Schaub's "managing the moment" framing: work with reactivity as information, not as an interruption).

## Failure modes

- **Avoidance culture ("conflict = community failure").** Early signs: parking-lot conversations, factional dinners, members skipping meetings, sudden quiet resignations. Practitioner response: normalize conflict as information (Schaub's "fight, flight, or opportunity" framing); leaders visibly use the conflict path on their own disputes.
- **NVC-speak as a weapon.** Correcting others' grammar of feeling, using "I feel that you…" formulations to smuggle in judgments, demanding others perform OFNR before being heard. Response: treat NVC as self-discipline, never a rule imposed on others (Kashtan).
- **Endless processing, no decisions.** Community becomes a therapy substitute; the same conflict is "processed" for months while decisions stall. Response: circles end in agreed action with a review date (Barter's post-circle); governance decisions stay in governance.
- **No consequences ladder.** One especially-challenging member consumes the community's energy for years because the only options are tolerance or expulsion. Response: Christian's graduated consequences, adopted before they're needed.
- **Misdiagnosis: mediating a structural problem.** Endless mediations between the treasurer and everyone else, when the real issue is no financial transparency. Ask first: "what missing system is producing this fight?" (Christian).
- **Founder/landlord power imbalance.** When one person owns the land, "conflict resolution" happens in the shadow of eviction power; NVC cannot equalize that (Kashtan's critique; Christian's ownership warnings). Response: move toward equalized tenure or explicit counterweights.
- **Intervening too late on the ladder.** Waiting until members are recruiting coalitions (Glasl stage 4+) and then attempting a friendly chat. Response: outside professional facilitation once side-taking appears. FIC maintains consultant referrals.

## When NOT to use this

- **Abuse, violence, harassment, or safety threats.** Mediation and circles presume rough equality and good faith; using them for abuse re-victimizes and can create legal exposure. These need protective action, clear boundaries, and possibly authorities. Not dialogue. (Widely held practitioner position; see also Kashtan on power differentials.)
- **Acute mental-health crises or addiction.** Community process is not treatment; route to professionals, and use the consequences track for behavior regardless of cause (Christian's "especially challenging behaviors").
- **Non-consenting parties.** Circles and NVC are voluntary by definition; a forced circle is a demand wearing a request's clothes. If someone declines the process repeatedly, that itself moves things to the agreements/consequences track.
- **Decisions masquerading as conflicts.** If the group actually needs to *decide* something (budget, membership, land use), use the governance process; empathy work complements but never replaces a decision method.
- Note for legal-adjacent situations (expulsion, tenancy, harassment claims): community agreements do not override law; rules vary by jurisdiction. This file is orientation, not legal advice.
