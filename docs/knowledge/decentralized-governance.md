# Decentralized & Distributed Governance

> How communities share power without either a boss or a mess: Ostrom's commons principles, sociocracy/holacracy circle systems, community land trust boards, polycentric (nested) governance, and the honest lessons from DAOs. A founder reaches for this when the community has outgrown "we just talk it out," when consensus is gridlocking, when one person or clique is quietly running everything, or when they're deciding how much structure a new village actually needs. Note: where this touches land tenure or legal entities (e.g., community land trusts), it is orientation, not legal advice — rules vary by jurisdiction; consult a local attorney.

## Core patterns

**1. Ostrom's eight design principles — the baseline checklist for any commons.**
Elinor Ostrom (Nobel 2009, *Governing the Commons*) studied communities that successfully managed shared resources for generations. The recurring features: (1) clearly defined boundaries — who is a member, what is the shared resource; (2) rules matched to local conditions, with costs proportional to benefits; (3) those affected by rules can participate in changing them; (4) monitoring by members or people accountable to members; (5) graduated sanctions — small first offenses get small consequences, not expulsion; (6) cheap, fast, local conflict-resolution; (7) outside authorities respect the community's right to self-organize; (8) nested layers for anything larger than one group. WHY it matters: this is empirical, not ideological — it's what actually endured. A 15-year study of Indiana intentional communities using Ostrom's framework found the same variables (collective-choice rules, monitoring, sanctioning, social capital) predicted robustness. Use the eight as an audit: most struggling communities are missing #1 (fuzzy membership), #4–5 (no monitoring, no proportionate consequences), or #6 (no conflict path short of blowup). (Ostrom, *Governing the Commons*; Ostrom Workshop, Indiana University; Christopher Allen's revised principles, Life With Alacrity.)

**2. Polycentric governance / subsidiarity — many centers, not one center or none.**
Ostrom's answer to "centralize or decentralize?" is: neither — have multiple decision-making centers at different scales, each with real authority over its own sphere, coordinating rather than commanding. Practically: the kitchen team decides kitchen things, the land-stewardship circle decides grazing, the whole community decides membership and budget. Decisions sit as close as possible to the people affected (subsidiarity). WHY: it keeps meetings short, keeps expertise where it's used, and prevents both bottleneck-leaders and 40-person debates about the compost bin. The failure to nest is why growth breaks flat communities (see Dancing Rabbit, below). (Ostrom Workshop; International Journal of the Commons.)

**3. Consent over consensus — sociocracy's core move.**
Sociocracy (a.k.a. Dynamic Governance) organizes a community into linked circles, each with a domain, making decisions by *consent*: a proposal passes unless someone has a *reasoned objection* — evidence it would harm the aim — rather than requiring everyone to positively agree. Objections are treated as information that improves the proposal, not vetoes. Circles connect via **double-linking**: a leader appointed downward and a delegate elected upward both sit in the parent circle, so information flows both ways. Decisions get **term limits and review dates** — "good enough for now, safe enough to try," revisited on schedule. WHY: pure consensus gives every individual a block, and practitioner experience across ecovillages is blunt about the result — demoralization and gridlock. Sociocracy is now the fastest-growing governance method in ecovillages and cohousing (full consensus remains the historical default), and practitioners report dramatic throughput gains ("more decisions in two months than the past two years" — Pioneer Valley Cohousing). (Sociocracy For All; Diana Leafe Christian; Global Ecovillage Network.)

**4. Holacracy — roles, not people; more structure, more overhead.**
Holacracy is sociocracy's more codified cousin: authority lives in precisely defined *roles* with explicit accountabilities, governed by a detailed constitution, with strict meeting formats (tactical vs. governance meetings). WHY a founder should know it: the role-not-person idea is genuinely useful — it depersonalizes power ("the Treasurer role decides X" vs. "Dana decides X") and makes handoffs clean. Practitioner consensus: holacracy was designed and priced for businesses; it is more expensive to learn and more rigid than most communities need. Common advice in the communities movement: borrow the role clarity, adopt sociocracy's lighter framework. (HolacracyOne / *Holacracy*, Brian Robertson; Enlivening Edge; Sociocracy For All comparison.)

**5. Community land trust governance — the tripartite board.**
The classic CLT is a membership nonprofit holding land in trust, governed by a board of one-third residents on trust land (elected by residents), one-third broader-community members, one-third public-interest/technical seats. WHY: it hard-wires a balance between the people who live on the land and accountability to the wider mission (e.g., permanent affordability), preventing both landlord-capture and resident-capture (residents alone might vote to privatize the equity). Practical reality: startups commonly run an interim board until there are enough residents to fill seats, and some newer CLTs drop the tripartite form when it doesn't fit their founding — it's a pattern, not a law of nature. This is orientation, not legal advice; CLT structure and nonprofit law vary by jurisdiction. (Grounded Solutions Network — CLT Technical Manual & Startup CLT Hub; Shelterforce.)

**6. DAO lessons that transfer offline — mostly warnings, some tools.**
DAOs ran a decade-long, well-documented experiment in leaderless governance. The data: voter turnout typically under 10%; the top 10% of token holders control ~76% of voting power; token-weighted voting reliably produces plutocracy (Compound's top 10 voters: ~58% of power). Recurrent failure dimensions named in the research: voter apathy, governance-as-performance, cognitive plutocracy, voter fatigue. What communities should copy anyway: **radical transparency** (every proposal, budget, and decision in a public log anyone can read); **proposal templates** (problem, option chosen, cost, who decides, review date); **delegation** (letting members hand their voice on a topic to someone they trust, revocably — raises effective participation); **quadratic voting** for budget-priority polls (voters spread limited points, cost of piling on one option rises quadratically — surfaces breadth of support, not just intensity of the loudest); **conviction voting**'s core idea — sustained support over time counts more than a one-day brigade. What NOT to copy: one-token-one-vote (wealth-weighted anything), and the belief that a voting app substitutes for deliberation. (arXiv "Concave is the New Linear"; SSRN, Arden 2026; Frontiers in Blockchain; RadicalxChange on quadratic voting.)

**7. There is no structureless group — write the structure down.**
Jo Freeman's "The Tyranny of Structurelessness" (1970, women's movement) is the foundational warning: rejecting formal structure doesn't eliminate power, it hides it — informal elites emerge based on friendship networks, and they are unaccountable precisely because they officially don't exist. Research on flat organizations confirms the pattern: initial motivation boost, then collapse under ambiguity, often reproducing existing gender/race/class hierarchies. Decision rule for founders: if you can't point to a written answer for "who decides X, and how do we change that," you have informal governance, and informal governance favors the well-connected. (Jo Freeman, "The Tyranny of Structurelessness"; Citizen Science: Theory & Practice.)

**8. When hierarchy is actually right.**
Distributed governance is for *policy* — direction, budgets, membership, rules. It is often wrong for *operations*. Hierarchy (a single accountable decider) is the correct tool when: (a) **emergencies** — fire, flood, medical, safety incidents need a pre-designated incident lead, not a circle meeting; (b) **execution within agreed policy** — once the community consents to "build the barn per this budget," the build lead should direct the build; (c) **expertise-dominant domains** — bookkeeping, legal filings, septic design; consent on the goal, delegate the method; (d) **speed-critical, reversible, low-stakes calls** — decision cost must not exceed decision value. The sociocratic frame keeps this safe: hierarchy inside a mandate, granted by consent, term-limited, reviewed, revocable. The failure isn't having leaders; it's leaders whose authority is unbounded, permanent, or undiscussable. (Freeman; Get Lighthouse / flat-org research; Sociocracy For All on operational roles.)

## In practice

A small village (5–40 adults) adopting this, in order:

1. **Write the boundary first (Ostrom #1).** One page: who is a member, how someone becomes one, how someone leaves. Fuzzy membership poisons every later vote.
2. **Adopt consent as the default decision rule** for policy decisions. Script: present proposal → clarifying questions round → reaction round → objection round ("any reasoned objection this harms our aim?") → integrate objections or pass. Put a review date on everything.
3. **Start with two or three circles, not seven.** Typically: a General Circle (whole-community policy, budget, membership) plus 2–3 domain circles (e.g., Land & Buildings, Community Life, Finance/Legal) with written mandates: domain, authority, budget, term. Double-link each to the General Circle. Add circles only when an existing one is overloaded.
4. **Set the cadence.** Domain circles meet biweekly-to-monthly for 60–90 min; General Circle monthly; whole-community gathering (information, connection, big-ticket consent) monthly or quarterly. Rotate facilitator and notetaker as elected, term-limited roles — sociocracy elects roles by consent nomination, not volunteering-by-default.
5. **Make it legible (the DAO lesson worth keeping).** One shared, searchable decision log: every proposal, its status, who decided, and the review date. A member who missed three months should be able to catch up in an hour. Tools: a shared drive or wiki is fine; Loomio and similar async-consent tools work well for proposals between meetings.
6. **Pre-authorize hierarchy for emergencies and builds.** Name the incident lead and the spending threshold under which roles act without a meeting (e.g., "any role may spend up to $200 within its domain; emergencies suspend normal process, reviewed after").
7. **Install conflict-resolution before the first conflict (Ostrom #6)** — see the conflict domain doc; minimally: a named path (direct conversation → mediated conversation → circle-appointed panel) and graduated sanctions written down.
8. **Audit yearly against Ostrom's eight.** It takes one meeting and reliably surfaces the missing principle before it becomes a crisis. Sociocracy For All's free assessment tool covers the circle-health side.
9. **If holding land collectively**, study the CLT tripartite pattern even if you don't adopt it wholesale: some seats accountable to residents, some to the mission/wider community. Use an interim board while small. (Orientation only — entity choice and land law vary by jurisdiction; get local counsel.)

## Failure modes

- **Consensus gridlock / tyranny of the minority.** A few habitual blockers stop proposals most members want; attendance and morale collapse; younger members quietly exit. Earthaven Ecovillage lived this and eventually replaced blocking with supermajority voting: 85%+ yes passes; 50–85% sends supporters and opponents into solution-oriented meetings; if those fail, the original proposal returns needing 66% — sentiment and attendance recovered. Early signs: the same 1–2 people block repeatedly; people say "why bother proposing anything." Fix: switch from consensus to consent, or add a supermajority fallback with mandatory objection-hearing rounds first. (Foundation for Intentional Community; Diana Leafe Christian — who reports ~90% of community startups fail, governance and unclear agreements being leading causes.)
- **Outgrowing the all-hands meeting.** Past ~30–40 adults, whole-group decision-making silently becomes rule-by-whoever-still-attends. Dancing Rabbit hit this and moved to an elected seven-member Village Council with tiered powers (propose / review / recall / final say). Early signs: attendance under half of members; "the meeting people" vs. everyone else. Fix: nest (polycentric structure) or elect a council — but keep recall and review powers with the membership. (Foundation for Intentional Community.)
- **Informal elite / founder's syndrome.** No written structure, so the founders' friend group is the real government (Freeman's warning), or a charismatic founder can't hand off. Early signs: decisions announced rather than proposed; newcomers can't explain how anything gets decided. Fix: write mandates, term-limit roles, elect by consent, double-link.
- **Participation collapse (the DAO disease).** Voting exists but under 10–20% engage; quorums fail; a motivated clique wins everything by showing up. Early signs: proposals passing with single-digit participation. Fixes practitioners use: fewer, better-framed decisions (most things delegated to circles); revocable delegation for members who opt out; conviction-style patience requirements for big irreversible calls; never wealth- or seniority-weighted votes.
- **Structure theater.** Adopting full holacracy (or elaborate sociocracy) overhead in a 10-person community: constitution debates replace actual work. Early sign: more time governing than doing. Fix: minimum viable structure — consent rule + 2–3 circles + decision log; grow structure only at pain points.
- **No graduated sanctions.** The only enforcement options are "ignore it" or "expel them," so nothing is enforced until an explosion. Fix: Ostrom #5 — written ladder: reminder → conversation → restitution/loss of privilege → membership review.
- **Resident-capture or mission-capture of land governance.** Residents alone vote themselves the commons' equity, or an outside-dominated board steamrolls residents. The CLT tripartite balance exists precisely because both happen. (Grounded Solutions; Shelterforce.)

## When NOT to use this

- **Crisis and safety operations.** Do not run consent rounds during a fire. Pre-agree command roles; debrief democratically afterward.
- **Very early stage (fewer than ~6 committed people).** A founding group needs shared vision documents and a simple consent rule, not circles. Build structure when coordination pain appears, not before.
- **Execution details inside an agreed mandate.** Once policy is consented, let the role work. Governing every operational choice is the fastest route to burnout.
- **When the group hasn't defined membership.** Distributed voting with fuzzy membership invites capture by whoever shows up. Boundary first.
- **As a substitute for conflict work.** Governance process cannot metabolize interpersonal rupture; a perfect consent process with unresolved resentment still deadlocks. Route to the conflict-resolution track.
- **Token/wealth-weighted voting, ever.** The DAO evidence is unambiguous: it converges on plutocracy. One member, one voice (with delegation if wanted).
