# Membership Design & Internal Economics

> How intentional communities decide who joins, how people become full members, how they leave, and how money and labor flow inside the community. A founder reaches for this domain when: strangers start asking to join; the founding circle needs to formalize "who is a member"; work is landing unevenly; or the community must set dues, joining fees, or an internal exchange system. The strong practitioner consensus (Diana Leafe Christian, FIC, Twin Oaks, GEN) is that membership design is the single highest-leverage screen against community failure — and that exit design must be written *before* anyone needs it. For anything touching fees, equity, refunds, or expulsion: this is orientation, not legal advice, and rules vary by jurisdiction — consult a local attorney before adopting binding agreements.

## Core patterns

**1. Select for emotional maturity; a high bar attracts healthier people.**
Diana Leafe Christian (*Creating a Life Together*, New Society Publishers) argues that communities with demanding entry processes attract people willing to work for belonging, while "wide open" doors disproportionately attract people seeking community as therapy — a dynamic she links to her estimate that ~90% of forming communities fail. Screen for: confidence, ability to ask directly for what they need, track record of getting along with others. Treat the group's *uncomfortable gut feeling* about an applicant as data, not rudeness to suppress. One mis-admitted member can consume the group's energy or (her Pueblo Encantada case study) trigger lawsuits that dissolve the community entirely.

**2. Stage the joining journey: visitor → provisional → full, over 6–12 months.**
Christian: most long-lived communities use an engagement period of six months to a year — long enough to see behavior under stress, not just best-visit behavior. Reference models:
- *Twin Oaks (VA, income-sharing, ~100 people)*: 3-week visitor program → mandatory 30 days away while the community decides (and the applicant reconsiders) → 6-month provisional membership with most rights but no vote → full-membership poll.
- *Earthaven (NC ecovillage)*: Supporting member (small monthly fee, attends meetings, no vote) → Provisional (min. 6 months, joining fee, meeting-attendance and work-hour requirements, may participate but not block) → Full member by community consensus.
- *Meadowdance*: explicit checkpoints at 1, 4, 7, and 13 months.
The "time away" step is underused and valuable: it forces a sober decision on both sides.

**3. Make acceptance an explicit decision with a defined threshold — never a default.**
Twin Oaks polls all members at the 6-month mark with graded options (accept / accept-with-feedback / extend / reject). Under 10% concern = accepted; 10–15% = feedback or a 3-month extension; 15%+ reject triggers a structured feedback process before any final poll. The load-bearing idea: quantified thresholds plus a feedback loop mean no one is admitted by silence and no one is rejected by an unaccountable clique. Small communities can scale this down (e.g., consent round with a named "pause" option) but should keep the principle: a scheduled decision point with a known rule. (Twin Oaks Membership Policy, twinoaks.org)

**4. Say no early; it is the kind option.**
Christian: declining an applicant during the process is mildly painful; expelling an established resident is traumatic and sometimes legally dangerous. Provisional members at Twin Oaks can be asked to leave at any time for any reason; full members only for enumerated grounds (abuse, theft, persistent work avoidance, bad-faith property declarations, attempts to destroy the community) via a public meeting where the accused responds. Two-tier protection is the pattern: easy exit during trial, strong due process after.

**5. Design the exit before the first entry.**
Practitioner consensus (Christian; FIC course "Designing a Community Membership Process"): write departure agreements while everyone still likes each other. Elements seen in mature communities:
- *Twin Oaks Leaving Fund*: a budgeted cash grant paid to any departing full member — de-escalates exits and prevents members feeling trapped by poverty.
- *Financial clarity on contributions*: common rule — money a member puts into shared property is either a documented loan (repayable on defined terms) or a documented gift, decided at the time of the expense, never left ambiguous (icmatch.org guidelines). Earthaven keeps 1/3 of the joining fee if a provisional member withdraws — refund rules stated up front.
- *Limited-equity mechanics*: housing co-ops (LEHC model) cap resale at an indexed formula and pre-approve which improvements are reimbursable. Departing-member payouts are the #1 source of community-killing lawsuits when undefined.
- FIC recommends exit interviews and an explicit grieving/closure practice — departures are emotional events for the whole group, not just paperwork.

**6. Twin Oaks labor-credit system: the reference design for full income-sharing.**
One hour of any work = one labor credit; quota ~42 hrs/week covers *all* work including cooking, cleaning, childcare — deliberately valuing domestic labor equally. Weekly preference cards → assigners build schedules → self-reported labor sheets (trust-based, one decimal place, small bonus for on-time submission). Over-quota work banks as vacation; members 50+ accrue pension credits reducing quota with age; members can transfer credits to each other (personal service credits) or pool them for projects. In exchange, the community covers housing, food, healthcare, spending allowance. Documented vulnerability: the whole system runs on honesty — "nobody is keeping close track of anybody else's work" — and complexity accretes over decades. (Federation of Egalitarian Communities, thefec.org; twinoaks.org)

**7. Internal currency: Dancing Rabbit's ELM system, the reference for non-income-sharing exchange.**
Dancing Rabbit (MO ecovillage) runs Exchange Local Money — a LETS-style mutual-credit currency, 1:1 with the US dollar, tracked online. Members pay rent, utilities, and food in ELMs. Individuals cannot go negative (a deliberate legal-caution choice); approved community organizations *can* carry public negative balances, functioning as interest-free financing — over 100,000 ELMs circulate, effectively an internal credit union. Why it matters: it keeps value circulating internally and finances member businesses without banks. Warning for founders: mutual-credit systems have tax and securities implications that vary by jurisdiction — orientation only, get legal advice. (ic.org; ecovillage.org)

**8. Dues and fees: three common architectures.**
- *Cohousing/HOA model*: annual budget (operations + reserve funding) divided across households; published California examples run $350–500/month; distinguish regular assessments from special assessments (unbudgeted, resented — a healthy reserve fund is the prevention). (cohousing.org; Fair Oaks EcoHousing)
- *Ecovillage model (Earthaven-style)*: one-time joining fee (Earthaven: ~$4,000; check current figures) + ongoing site-lease fees + labor requirement (e.g., 48 hrs/quarter). Joining fees fund infrastructure and test commitment.
- *Income-sharing model (Twin Oaks/FEC)*: no dues; all member income pools, labor quota replaces fees.
Rule of thumb from Christian: whatever the architecture, put every financial obligation in a signed written agreement — groups reliably "remember agreements differently."

**9. A standing membership team beats ad-hoc gatekeeping.**
FIC's course argues for a permanent membership team owning the funnel: outreach, visitor hosting, orientation, integration ("tending" new members after entry), and exit interviews. Twin Oaks rate-limits assimilation (max ~3 new members/month) so the culture can absorb newcomers. Integration is a distinct phase — communities lose good members in year one by treating acceptance as the finish line.

**10. Participation systems in non-income-sharing communities: equitable, not equal.**
Cohousing practice ranges from quantified minimums (cook one common meal/month + serve on a committee) to un-quantified "participate as best you can" norms. The trend (Cohousing Association) is away from rigid hour-counting toward *equitable* contribution matched to ability — with a controversial "buyout" option (pay instead of work) that some communities allow and others reject as corrosive to culture. Whichever is chosen, name it explicitly; the silent alternative is the martyr/slacker spiral (FIC's term).

## In practice

**First 90 days for a forming community:**
1. Write the vision/values doc and the membership agreement *before* advertising for members. Include: stages, duration, fees, labor expectation, decision rule for acceptance, exit terms, refund rules.
2. Stand up a membership team of 2–3 people; one is the named contact for every inquiry.
3. Define the visit: length (a weekend minimum; 1–3 weeks for residential), what visitors do (work alongside members — work reveals character faster than meetings), and a structured debrief on both sides.
4. Set the provisional period at 6–12 months with a calendar-scheduled decision point and graded outcomes (accept / extend / decline). Extending is normal, not an insult — say so up front.
5. Charge something at every stage (even a small supporting-member fee): it filters for commitment and funds the process.
6. Write the exit clause now: what a departing member gets back, on what timeline, and who decides. Have a lawyer in your jurisdiction review anything touching property or equity.

**Cadence for an operating community:**
- Poll or consent-round on every provisional member at the scheduled date — never let it slide.
- Annual budget process sets dues/quota; publish it; fund reserves to avoid special assessments.
- Quarterly check on labor balance: who is over, who is under, is it becoming a story people tell.
- Exit interview for every departure, and a closure ritual for significant ones.

**Tools:** written labor preference forms, a shared ledger for any internal credit system (public balances for organizational accounts, per Dancing Rabbit), a membership pipeline tracker, signed agreements stored where everyone can find them.

## Failure modes

- **Admitting from desperation.** Under-populated communities lower the bar; the wrong member then consumes more energy than the empty room did. Early sign: the group overrides someone's expressed discomfort "because we need people." Fix: recruit harder, screen the same.
- **Acceptance by drift.** No scheduled decision point → the long-term guest who is somehow a member with no agreement. Early sign: "wait, did we ever actually decide about them?" Fix: calendar-driven polls, graded outcomes.
- **Undocumented money.** Contributions to land/buildings with no loan-vs-gift paper. Surfaces at exit as a lawsuit (Pueblo Encantada: forced property sale, community dissolved). Fix: classify every contribution in writing at the time it's made.
- **Martyr/slacker spiral.** No explicit participation norm → a few over-function, resent, burn out; others under-function and are quietly hated. Early sign: workload complaints in the parking lot, not the meeting. Fix: explicit expectations, regular labor review, FIC-style "difficult conversations about performance" as a normal governance item.
- **Trust-system gaming.** Self-reported labor and mutual credit run on honesty; Twin Oaks names rounding-up, double-crediting, and sick-hour padding as known abuses. Fix: transparency (public-ish records), cultural norm-setting, and enumerated consequences up to expulsion — not surveillance.
- **Two-class ownership.** Owner-landlord plus tenant-members creates what Christian calls feudal dynamics: consensus theater the owner can override. Fix: real power-sharing structures (co-op shares, land trust, LLC membership), or honestly don't market it as community.
- **Exit as taboo.** No leaving fund, no refund schedule, no exit interview → members stay resentfully because leaving is unaffordable, or leave explosively. Fix: budget for graceful exits; make leaving a legitimate, discussed option.
- **Currency/complexity creep.** Internal economies accrete special cases ("all economic systems get complicated over years of use" — Twin Oaks manual). Fix: periodic simplification review; sunset rules nobody can explain.

## When NOT to use this

- **Full income-sharing (Twin Oaks model):** don't adopt it for groups that haven't lived together, hold unequal outside assets/debts they intend to keep, or lack a shared on-site livelihood. It demands near-total economic trust and a labor bureaucracy; most communities are better served by dues + participation norms.
- **Internal currency:** skip it below roughly 30–50 active adults — the ledger overhead exceeds the benefit; informal reciprocity works. Also skip where members have no goods/services to trade with each other.
- **Heavy multi-stage vetting:** overkill for non-residential projects (a community garden, a coordination platform group) where the cost of a bad member is low and exit is trivial. Match screening depth to entanglement depth: shared *ownership* justifies the most; shared *events* the least.
- **Quantified labor quotas:** poor fit for cohousing-style communities of households with outside jobs and wide ability ranges; the trend there is equitable-contribution norms, not hour-counting.
- **Joining fees:** if the mission requires economic diversity, high fees screen out exactly who you want; use sliding scales, sweat-equity alternatives, or lending circles instead — but still charge *something*.
- **Any of the financial/expulsion mechanics without local legal review:** membership agreements interact with landlord-tenant law, securities law (for internal currencies and member loans), and nonprofit/co-op statutes differently in every jurisdiction. This document is orientation, not legal advice.
