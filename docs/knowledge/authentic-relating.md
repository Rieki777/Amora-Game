# Authentic Relating & Relational Culture

> Practices that build a community's "trust muscle" deliberately instead of hoping it accretes: Authentic Relating (AR) games, Circling, ZEGG Forum, and their T-group ancestors. A founder reaches for this when the group's technical coordination is fine but the relational field is thin (surface politeness, unspoken resentments, conflict that goes underground), or proactively, because every durable community treats connection as scheduled infrastructure, not a byproduct. This domain is about *culture practices*; it is not therapy, not conflict mediation, and not governance, and it fails when asked to substitute for any of those.

## Core patterns

**1. Trust is built on a cadence, not assumed.** Communities that last treat relational practice like exercise: regular, structured, low-stakes reps before high-stakes moments. ZEGG (Germany) calls its Forum practice "the glue that keeps community alive and together" and stresses it only works "when you practice it continuously within the context of a group that is more or less stable" (zegg-forum.org; CLIPS/GEN-Europe). One-off workshops produce a glow, not a culture.

**2. Know the lineage: it carries both the tools and the warnings.** The modern field descends from Kurt Lewin's first T-group (1946, Connecticut) and the National Training Laboratories (Bethel, Maine, 1947): small unstructured groups learning from here-and-now interaction and peer feedback (Wikipedia: T-groups; Ed Batista, "A Brief History of T-Groups"). The 1960s-70s encounter-group wave scaled this up and produced the field's foundational safety research (see pattern 8). Circling emerged in the late-1990s Bay Area (Guy Sengstock and Jerry Candelaria's Areté Experience, 1998) and branched into several schools; Authentic Relating grew alongside it as the open-source, games-based cousin (Circling Guide, "History of Circling"). Stanford GSB still teaches the T-group directly as "Interpersonal Dynamics."

**3. The Five Practices are the shared grammar.** ART International's canon: *Welcome Everything* (meet what arises without judgment), *Assume Nothing* (check assumptions with curiosity), *Reveal Your Experience* (let yourself be seen), *Own Your Experience* (your feelings and impact are yours), *Honor Self and Other* (balance your needs with theirs) (authenticrelating.co, "The Five Practices"). These five double as everyday community norms, usable in meetings, conflict, and onboarding as much as at practice nights.

**4. AR games isolate one relational skill inside a tight structure.** A game gives explicit rules, a time box, and a narrow focus, which makes vulnerability *optional and graduated*. Canonical examples: the **Noticing Game** ("Sitting here with you, I notice…": present-moment awareness), **Curiosity** (one person only asks genuine questions), **Hot Seat** (one person fields any question, with the right to pass). Sara Ness's *Authentic Relating Games Manual* (Authentic Revolution) collects 150+ games and is the field's standard playbook. Structure is why games are the safest entry point for newcomers.

**5. Circling is the emergent, deeper practice, and needs a skilled facilitator.** Circling is "un-withheld, present-moment connection": a small group attends to one person's moment-to-moment world (a "birthday circle") or to whatever arises in the group with no designated focus ("organic" or "surrendered-leadership" circling) (Circling Guide). Rule of thumb from practitioners: AR games are structured and teachable by a competent peer; Circling is emergent and amplifies whatever the facilitator brings, so sequence games first, Circling later, with trained leadership (Circling Institute; Circling Guide, "Circling vs. Authentic Relating"). Note: "Circling" was trademarked (c. 2020) by a group of the lineage schools; "Authentic Relating" remains open-source.

**6. ZEGG Forum is the community-scale format.** Developed at ZEGG and Tamera specifically to build transparency in residential communities: the group sits in a circle; one person ("presenter") works an issue in the center with a trained facilitator; others then offer "mirrors": what they saw and felt, given with goodwill. Works best at 15-35 participants, practiced regularly with a stable group; it explicitly is *not* a decision-making body (zegg-forum.org; CLIPS/GEN-Europe). For a village, this is the format that scales beyond a living room.

**7. Agreements make the container.** Every functioning format runs on explicit, restated agreements: confidentiality ("stories stay, lessons leave"), speak from "I," ownership of experience, the unconditional right to pass, no advice or fixing unless requested, and consent before feedback. The agreements, not the facilitator's charisma, are what let people risk honesty. Restate them every session; newcomers hear them for the first time, veterans need the reminder.

**8. Facilitation style is the main safety variable.** The landmark study *Encounter Groups: First Facts* (Lieberman, Yalom & Miles, 1973) found roughly one in ten participants harmed, and that casualties clustered under a specific leader type: high emotional stimulation, aggressive, charismatic, intrusive. Two further findings every community should internalize: leaders were poor judges of who got hurt (*peers* detected casualties reliably), and the most vulnerable participants were those with low self-esteem and high expectations of transformation. Translation: prefer calm, low-drama facilitation; build peer check-out channels; watch newcomers who arrive hungry for breakthrough.

**9. Relational practice and governance are separate channels.** Practitioners consistently warn against letting Forum/Circling become where decisions happen or where conflict is prosecuted. The practice surfaces material; governance (sociocracy, consensus, whatever you use) and formal conflict process act on it. ZEGG states this directly for Forum. Blurring the two produces both bad decisions and unsafe practice space.

## In practice

**Starting sequence for a small village (first 6 months):**
1. **Weeks 1-8:** a weekly or biweekly 90-120 min AR games night, 6-12 people. Arc of a session: opening check-in → warmup game (Noticing in pairs) → one deeper game (Curiosity, Hot Seat) → closing round + agreements review. Any steady, humble member can host from the Games Manual.
2. **Months 2-4:** send 2-3 members (the founder plus others) to formal training: ART International courses, a Circling school (Circling Institute, Circling Europe, The Relateful Company), or ZEGG Forum facilitator training (offered in several countries). A 6-week cohort course like Beneteau's "Authentic Relating in Community" (ARC) is a proven on-ramp for whole groups (circlingguide.com/arc).
3. **Months 4-6+:** add a committed closed group (same 4-8 people, weekly) for Circling/relational meditation, and, once the residential group is stable and >15 people, a monthly Forum.

**Closed and committed beats drop-in.** The best-documented community case study (Ajijic, Mexico; circlingguide.com, 2023) found open public circles fizzled while committed returning groups of 4-6 thrived; two communities there adopted AR as a core modality by embedding it in existing meetings. Same finding as ZEGG: stability of membership is the active ingredient.

**Pay or formally recognize facilitators.** Ajijic sustained peer leaders at $40-60/evening; unpaid facilitation burns out precisely the people holding the container. Budget for it like you budget for tool maintenance.

**Membership integration, the honest version.** Make participation in relational practice an *explicit, stated* expectation of the trial-membership period (e.g., "attend games night at least 2×/month during your 6-month trial"), so prospective members self-select and the community sees how someone handles feedback before granting full membership. Diana Leafe Christian's *Creating a Life Together*, the standard manual on membership process, argues for exactly this kind of structured, criteria-based joining process. Two rules keep it clean: (a) tell candidates the practice is part of how the community gets to know them, and never use it as a covert evaluation; (b) membership decisions still run through the governance process on stated criteria, with the practice as one input, not a verdict delivered in-circle.

**Everyday micro-doses.** The cheapest wins: a 60-second personal check-in round opening every work meeting; "impact rounds" at the end of hard meetings; the Five Practices posted as house norms. Communities report this does more for baseline trust than monthly deep-dives.

## Failure modes

- **Charismatic-intensity facilitation.** The Lieberman/Yalom/Miles casualty profile: a magnetic leader escalating emotion, pushing breakthroughs. Early signs: sessions routinely running to tears as a success metric, facilitator centrality, pressure framing ("you're holding back"). Fix: rotate facilitation, adopt low-stimulation norms, and give the facilitator role explicit term limits.
- **Coerced vulnerability.** The right to pass erodes; not sharing is read as resistance; "authenticity" becomes compulsory performance. Early sign: people rehearsing disclosures to stay in good standing. Fix: restate opt-outs every session, celebrate passes, keep games graduated.
- **Processing culture / governance bypass.** Meetings dissolve into feelings; decisions stall; the loudest processors gain de facto power. Fix: separate calendars. Practice space has no agenda items; business meetings time-box check-ins.
- **AR grammar weaponized in conflict.** "I notice I'm feeling attacked by you" as a prosecution dressed in ownership language. Practitioners flag this as the most common corruption. Fix: real conflicts route to a named conflict process (mediation, restorative circle), not to games night.
- **In-group/out-group split around the practice.** The Ajijic case study documents resentment when a hand-picked inner cohort formed around the training; "questions of money, power and prestige" required intervention. Fix: transparent selection for trainings, public pathway for anyone to join, and leadership naming the dynamic early. Beneteau's blunt summary: AR "changes the rules of relationship" and *will* generate resistance. Plan for it.
- **Cult drift.** Warning signs practitioners and critics converge on (wouter.org "Cult or not"; LessWrong discussions of Circling): a guru-ized facilitator, intimacy escalation as status, discouraging outside relationships or outside critique, practice claims expanding into total life-philosophy. Fix: multiple lineage inputs, external facilitator exchanges, and normalized exit.
- **Invisible harm.** Research shows facilitators miss casualties; harmed people often just feel vague unease and quietly withdraw. Early sign: someone stops attending after an intense session. Fix: buddy check-outs 24-48h after deep sessions, and a stated norm that anyone can flag concern about anyone's state to a designated steward.
- **One-off workshop syndrome.** A weekend intensive with no ongoing container produces intimacy spikes, "vulnerability hangovers," and no culture change. ZEGG: one-time application yields minimal benefit. Fix: never run an intensive without the weekly cadence already standing.

## When NOT to use this

- **Active acute conflict.** Use mediation or a restorative process with a neutral third party; putting a live dispute in a circle asymmetrically exposes whoever is more vulnerable.
- **Mental-health crisis or acute trauma.** These practices are peer practices, not therapy; facilitators are not clinicians. Refer out, and say so in the group agreements.
- **Decision-making.** Forum/Circling surface information; they do not decide. If a "decision" emerges in-circle, it still goes through governance.
- **Covert vetting.** Never run relational practice as a hidden membership test or use in-circle disclosures against a candidate; it poisons the container for everyone.
- **Unnamed power differentials.** Landowner-and-residents, employer-and-employees, founder-and-newcomers: deep practice under unacknowledged power asymmetry manufactures compliance, not trust. Name the asymmetry or keep the practice shallow.
- **Captive audiences.** If people cannot realistically opt out (housing or income depends on standing), participation must be genuinely voluntary with zero standing consequences. Otherwise skip it.
